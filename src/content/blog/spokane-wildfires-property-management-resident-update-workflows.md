Spokane just showed why "we'll update residents after the staff meeting" is not a wildfire workflow.

That is the operating lesson property managers should take from the wildfire emergency around Spokane on August 2 and August 3, 2026.

On August 3, AP reported that the fires in and around Spokane had destroyed at least 600 structures, forced the evacuation of 60,000 people, and remained 0% contained on Monday morning. AP also reported that local authorities had opened a shelter at Spokane's convention center, where about 400 people were staying, while schools launched emergency child-care support. A few days earlier, the National Weather Service had issued a Particularly Dangerous Situation red flag warning for the Spokane area, underscoring how quickly conditions could move from high risk to active disruption.

EMC2Ops builds done-for-you AI front desk workflows for property managers. The point of this story is not climate commentary and it is not generic AI coverage. The point is that wildfire conditions expose the same operational weakness many portfolios already have: resident communication, damage intake, vendor coordination, owner visibility, and CRM or PMS logging still split across voicemail, texts, portal threads, and staff memory.

## Why property managers should care

Wildfire disruption is not just a maintenance problem. It is a routing and communication problem.

One resident calls to ask whether their building is under evacuation notice. Another texts photos of smoke damage and wants to know whether a maintenance request was opened. A third asks if a scheduled move-in is still happening because access roads may close before evening. Meanwhile, the on-call manager is trying to sort which issues are life-safety questions, which are damage-intake tasks, and which require an owner update right now.

That is why the broad [how to automate property management](/use-cases/how-to-automate-property-management/) page is the right money-page anchor here. Operators with 50+ doors need one workflow that can acknowledge the issue, capture the relevant facts once, route the next safe step, keep residents informed, and update the system of record. This is also why [property management maintenance intake automation](/blog/property-management-maintenance-intake-automation/) and [property management resident portal message automation](/blog/property-management-resident-portal-message-automation/) belong to the same operating question.

## What this wildfire story does not mean

It does not mean EMC2Ops is integrated with AP, the National Weather Service, Spokane emergency management, or any wildfire response platform.

It does not mean a property manager should let automation decide whether a resident should evacuate, whether a building is habitable, or whether a situation creates legal or insurance exposure.

The narrower lesson is more useful: during a fast-moving disruption, repetitive coordination work has to move faster and more consistently than a human-only inbox model usually can. The right response is a controlled workflow, similar to [the AI front desk loop](/blog/ai-front-desk-loop-not-chatbot/), that can acknowledge, collect, summarize, route, update, and stop when human judgment is required.

## The operational expectation that changes during an evacuation event

Residents judge the office by whether the next useful update arrives when conditions change.

If a resident believes smoke is affecting the building, "we received your message" is not enough. They want to know whether the team understands the property and unit involved, whether someone is reviewing the case, and when they should expect the next update. That is why [property management maintenance status update automation](/blog/property-management-maintenance-status-update-automation/) matters more than a vague promise to improve communication. Under disruption pressure, speed without clarity just creates a second wave of calls and texts.

## The workflow to fix first

Start with wildfire-related resident update and damage-intake routing tied to vendor handoffs, owner visibility, and CRM or PMS write-back.

The workflow should look like this:

1. A resident calls, texts, or sends a portal message about evacuation status, smoke or fire damage, blocked access, utility issues, or move-in disruption.
2. The system sends an approved acknowledgement immediately instead of leaving a silence gap.
3. It captures the minimum useful fields: property, building, unit or area, callback number, issue type, current access status, whether evacuation instructions were already received, photos if available, and any vulnerable-resident or safety context.
4. It flags cases that require immediate human review based on approved emergency and escalation rules.
5. It drafts a clean vendor or field-team handoff with scope, access notes, resident contact information, urgency markers, and the latest status.
6. It sends the resident a real next-step update instead of making them ask again.
7. It writes the summary and current state back to the CRM or PMS so the overnight team and the morning team are looking at one record.

## What to automate

Automate the repetitive work that reduces delay and confusion:

- immediate acknowledgement across calls, texts, and resident portal messages
- intake prompts for property, unit, callback number, evacuation status, damage type, photos, and access restrictions
- move-in or scheduled-visit disruption capture when access or timing changes
- vendor-summary drafting with complete handoff fields
- resident update messages when the case is assigned, waiting, scheduled, delayed, or closed
- owner-summary drafting when a property needs visibility beyond the resident thread
- CRM or PMS logging after each material step
- morning rollups for overnight wildfire-related activity

These tasks fit naturally with [property management automation tasks](/blog/property-management-automation-tasks/), [reduce administrative workload in property management](/blog/reduce-administrative-workload-property-management/), and [property management owner approval workflow](/blog/property-management-owner-approval-workflow/). A disruption event does not just increase message volume. It multiplies context-switching cost.

## What not to automate

Keep humans in charge of:

- life-safety emergencies
- evacuation and re-entry judgment
- habitability decisions
- accommodations and vulnerable-resident situations
- complaints
- lease interpretation
- approvals with financial consequences
- owner exception decisions
- any case where low confidence changes the safety, legal, or insurance risk

That boundary matters for the same reason [property management repair approval automation](/blog/property-management-repair-approval-automation/) and [property management maintenance escalation automation](/blog/property-management-maintenance-escalation-automation/) matter. Automation should remove repetitive coordination work, not make the hardest call.

## Metrics to track and rollout path

Do not measure success as "we used AI during the wildfire."

Measure whether the workflow actually improved operations:

- time to first useful wildfire-related response
- resident cases captured with complete evacuation or damage intake
- resident status update turnaround after assignment or delay
- vendor handoff completeness
- owner update turnaround
- CRM or PMS logging accuracy
- urgent cases escalated within policy
- morning admin time spent rebuilding overnight cases

The rollout path should stay narrow. Pick one disruption path first: evacuation-related resident updates, smoke or damage intake, or move-in disruption handling. Define the trigger, required fields, escalation rules, write-back behavior, and approved update templates. Review the misses after the first event, then expand.
